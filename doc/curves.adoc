[#crypto-curves]
== Curves: `webcpp::crypto::curves`

An *elliptic curve* gives public-key cryptography with short keys: a secret key
is a number, its public key a point of the curve computed from it, and going
back from the point to the number is infeasible. On a curve a program can
*sign*, so that anyone with the public key can check who wrote a message
(link:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signature[Wikipedia]), and *agree
on a key*, so that two parties who only exchanged public keys share a secret
(link:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic-curve_Diffie%E2%80%93Hellman[Wikipedia]).

The namespace `webcpp::crypto::curves` holds four curves in this version, the
columns of the table below. The first is secp256k1 (link:https://www.secg.org/sec2-v2.pdf[SEC 2];
link:https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Secp256k1[Bitcoin Wiki]), the curve of
Bitcoin, Ethereum and Nostr (<<crypto-secp256k1>>). This version covers
Bitcoin's keys and signatures, Nostr's event signatures (NIP-01) and the raw
x-coordinate ECDH of its encrypted messages (NIP-44, <<crypto-secp256k1-ecdh>>),
and Ethereum's ECDSA: recoverable signatures over SHA-256 or over Keccak-256,
Ethereum's own, the recovery of a signer's public key from a signature, and the
uncompressed key (<<crypto-secp256k1-recovery>>, <<crypto-secp256k1-ethereum>>).
Its functions, by header:

* `secp256k1.hpp`, the key pair and its signatures and ECDH: `keypair::from_secret`, `generate`,
and the key pair's `public_key`, `secret`, `sign` with `crypto::schnorr`,
`crypto::ecdsa_sha256`, `crypto::ecdsa_sha256_recoverable` and
`crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable`, `agree`,
`agree_hashed`, `agree_point` and `tweak_xonly`; the `public_key` type's `from_bytes`,
`compressed`, `uncompressed` and `xonly`; `crypto::verify` with `crypto::schnorr`
and `crypto::ecdsa_sha256`, `recover`, `to_der`, `from_der`, `normalize_low_s`,
`is_low_s` and the x-only key's `tweak_xonly`;
* `secp256k1/tweak.hpp`, tweaks of whole keys, the arithmetic of BIP 32's
derivation and of watch-only wallets: `seckey_tweak_add`, `seckey_tweak_mul`,
`seckey_negate`, `pubkey_tweak_add`, `pubkey_tweak_mul`, `pubkey_negate` and
`pubkey_combine` (<<crypto-secp256k1-tweaks>>);
* `secp256k1/formats.hpp`, keys in the SPKI and PKCS#8 files that OpenSSL
exchanges: `encode_spki`, `decode_spki`, `encode_pkcs8` and `decode_pkcs8`
(<<crypto-secp256k1-formats>>);
* `secp256k1/ellswift.hpp`, keys sent as random-looking bytes, BIP 324's
ElligatorSwift: `ellswift_create`, `ellswift_encode`, `ellswift_decode` and
`ellswift_xdh` (<<crypto-secp256k1-ellswift>>);
* `secp256k1/musig.hpp`, many signers' keys and signatures aggregated into
one, BIP 327's MuSig2: `key_aggregate`, `nonce_gen`, `nonce_aggregate`,
`nonce_process`, `partial_sign`, `partial_signature_verify`,
`partial_signature_aggregate`, and the `parse_` and `serialize_` functions of
what the signers send (<<crypto-secp256k1-musig>>);
* `secp256k1/silent_payments.hpp`, one reusable address whose payments look
unrelated, BIP 352's silent payments: `create_outputs`, `create_label`,
`create_labeled_spend_key`, `make_prevouts_summary` and `scan_outputs`
(<<crypto-secp256k1-silent-payments>>).

Ethereum's `v` encoding of the recovery id, the hash it signs, its
transactions and its addresses are a domain library's, and so is BIP 32's
derivation, its HMAC-SHA-512 and its paths, which only adds a tweak computed
there to a whole key
(link:https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0032.mediawiki[BIP 32]).
The second is Ed25519,
RFC 8032's signatures on edwards25519, with Ed25519ph for a message's SHA-512
or a message given in pieces (link:https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8032[RFC 8032];
<<crypto-ed25519>>). The third is X25519, RFC 7748's key agreement on
Curve25519, the same curve in another form, with the conversion of an Ed25519
key to it (link:https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7748[RFC 7748];
<<crypto-x25519>>), on which libsodium's key exchange, `crypto_kx`, is built
in `key_exchange` (<<crypto-key-exchange>>). The fourth is P-256, NIST's curve
of FIPS 186-5, the curve of browsers' WebCrypto, of passkeys and of TLS
certificates, on BearSSL: key pairs
made from a scalar or drawn, public keys validated once, ECDSA over SHA-256
with RFC 6979's nonce, signed low S, raw and DER, ECDH's raw x
coordinate, as WebCrypto's `deriveBits` gives it, and its keys as SPKI and
PKCS#8 (link:https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/186-5/final[FIPS 186-5];
<<crypto-p256>>, <<crypto-p256-ecdsa>>, <<crypto-p256-ecdh>>,
<<crypto-p256-formats>>). A program chooses a curve for compatibility: a signature on
one curve is checked only with that curve, and a browser's peer needs P-256.
Ed25519's, X25519's and P-256's keys leave a program, and enter it, in the standard forms
OpenSSL and WebCrypto use, SPKI and PKCS#8, as DER or PEM text (<<crypto-key-formats>>).
Every curve signs and verifies by the same two names, the scheme named at every
call, and its key pair keeps one contract (<<crypto-curves-signatures>>); each
operation has the member and its free form, and, where WebCrypto has the
algorithm (Ed25519 and P-256), their asynchronous forms
(<<crypto-curves-four-forms>>).

[#crypto-curves-signatures]
=== Signatures: the scheme and the input

`<webcpp/crypto/signing.hpp>` gives every curve one way to sign and one way to
verify. A key pair signs with `key.sign(scheme, input)`, and
`crypto::verify(scheme, signature, input, public_key)`, in the namespace
`webcpp::crypto`, checks a signature with the public key alone. The scheme is a
tag object that names the algorithm and, for ECDSA, its hash, as WebCrypto's
`+{name: "ECDSA", hash: "SHA-256"}+` does
(link:https://www.w3.org/TR/WebCryptoAPI/#ecdsa-operations[W3C, ECDSA's
operations]): `crypto::eddsa`, `crypto::eddsa_prehashed`,
`crypto::ecdsa_sha256`, `crypto::ecdsa_sha256_recoverable`,
`crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable` and `crypto::schnorr`. It is named at every
call, on every curve, so that the call says what it signs; a tag's constructor
is explicit, so that `+{}+` never stands for a scheme.

The input is a message or a digest, and the program says which:

* A *message* is any bytes that convert to `std::span<std::uint8_t const>`: a
span, a `std::array`, a `std::vector`, of any size, the empty one included.
ECDSA hashes it with SHA-256 inside the call, as WebCrypto does, and Ethereum's
`crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable` with Keccak-256; EdDSA and Schnorr sign it
as given; Ed25519ph, `crypto::eddsa_prehashed`, takes none.
* A *digest* is `+crypto::prehashed{digest}+`, made from a
`std::array<std::uint8_t, N>`, a span of the fixed size N, or a Boost.Hash2
digest such as the result of `webcpp::hash::sha2_256`. ECDSA signs it as
given, and so does Ed25519ph, `crypto::eddsa_prehashed`, a message's SHA-512.
The marker refers to its digest, as a span does, and must not outlive it.

The hazard this removes is the 32-byte message. A SHA-256 digest is 32 bytes,
and so is many a message: a key, a protocol's hash, a nonce. Where a function
takes a digest as a span of bytes, a 32-byte message passed to it compiles,
signs the message unhashed, and verifies under the same mistake, and a program
that moves from one function or one curve to another changes the meaning of
its argument without a diagnostic. Here a span is a message whatever its size,
and no conversion turns a span into a `crypto::prehashed` or a
`crypto::prehashed` into a span: a digest is a digest because the program says
so.

.The schemes, their keys and their inputs
[cols="2,3,2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| Scheme | Key pair; the public key `crypto::verify` takes | A message | `+crypto::prehashed{digest}+` | Signature

| `crypto::eddsa`
| Ed25519: `curves::ed25519::keypair`; `curves::ed25519::public_key`
| signed as given (RFC 8032's PureEdDSA)
| does not compile
| `curves::ed25519::signature`, 64 bytes

| `crypto::eddsa_prehashed`
| Ed25519: `curves::ed25519::keypair`; `curves::ed25519::public_key` (native backends)
| does not compile
| 64 bytes, the message's SHA-512 (RFC 8032's Ed25519ph)
| `curves::ed25519::signature`, 64 bytes, not interchangeable with a signature of `crypto::eddsa`

| `crypto::ecdsa_sha256`
| P-256: `curves::p256::keypair`; `curves::p256::public_key`
| hashed with SHA-256 inside the call
| 32 bytes, signed as given
| `curves::p256::ecdsa_signature`, 64 bytes, r then s, low S

| `crypto::ecdsa_sha256`
| secp256k1: `curves::secp256k1::keypair`; `curves::secp256k1::public_key`
| hashed with SHA-256 inside the call
| 32 bytes, signed as given
| `curves::secp256k1::ecdsa_signature`, 64 bytes, r then s, low S

| `crypto::ecdsa_sha256_recoverable`
| secp256k1: `curves::secp256k1::keypair`; none: `recover` gives the key
| hashed with SHA-256 inside the call
| 32 bytes, signed as given
| `curves::secp256k1::recoverable_signature`, 65 bytes, r, s and the recovery id

| `crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable`
| secp256k1: `curves::secp256k1::keypair`; none: `recover` gives the key
| hashed with Keccak-256 inside the call (`/third_party//xkcp` linked)
| 32 bytes, signed as given
| `curves::secp256k1::recoverable_signature`, 65 bytes, r, s and the recovery id

| `crypto::schnorr`
| secp256k1: `curves::secp256k1::keypair`; `curves::secp256k1::public_key`, or its 32-byte x-only form
| signed as given (BIP 340)
| does not compile
| `curves::secp256k1::schnorr_signature`, 64 bytes
|===

Every other pair stops the build in a `static_assert` whose message says why:
a scheme the key's curve does not have, `crypto::eddsa` or `crypto::schnorr`
with a marker, `crypto::eddsa_prehashed` with a message, a marker whose digest
is not the scheme's size (32 bytes, or Ed25519ph's 64), a marker made from a span
whose size the type does not know, a marker on a backend that cannot sign a
digest; <<crypto-jamfile-messages,the table of messages>> gives each with its
fix. The
concepts `crypto::can_sign<Keypair, Scheme, Input>` and
`crypto::can_verify<Scheme, Signature, Input, PublicKey>` give a program's own
templates the same answer. A public key's type says its curve: ECDSA's key is a
validated object on both curves, never bytes, so that `crypto::ecdsa_sha256`
never infers its curve from a number of bytes. BIP 340's keys are bytes on the
wire, so `crypto::schnorr` also takes the 32-byte x-only key.

The signature is a type of its curve and scheme, the table's last column:
`sign` returns it, and every `verify`, member and free, synchronous and
asynchronous, on every curve, and secp256k1's `recover` take it and nothing
else, so that a signature of another curve or scheme, or bytes, stops the
build with a message naming the type expected. Each is a value, copied and
compared with `==`, and gives its bytes with `bytes()`. A signature that
arrives from the network or a file is made first, with its type's
`from_bytes(bytes)` or `crypto::from_bytes<T>(bytes)`, from a `std::array`, a
`std::vector` or a span of a received buffer: its size alone is checked there,
and another size than the table's is `invalid_signature`; whether its numbers
are in range is the verification's judgment. Ed25519's two schemes share
`curves::ed25519::signature`, which Ed25519ph's signer gives too: the type does
not tell them apart, and each verifies only its own scheme's.

ECDSA signs low S on both curves, s at most (n - 1) / 2: libsecp256k1 signs
so, and P-256's RFC 6979 signature is normalized after it is made, n - s
selected by a mask, without a branch on the secret. Verification differs on
purpose. secp256k1's `crypto::verify` refuses a high S, as libsecp256k1 and
Bitcoin's policy do
(link:https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0146.mediawiki[BIP
146]); P-256's accepts both forms, as FIPS 186-5 and WebCrypto do, whose own
signatures may be high. A protocol that requires low S from a P-256 peer
checks `is_low_s` before `crypto::verify` (<<crypto-p256-ecdsa>>).

DER, low S and the 64-byte form are formats of a signature, not schemes, and
they need the curve's order: each ECDSA curve's namespace has the same four
functions. `to_der(signature, output)` writes strict DER into at least
`max_der_size` bytes and returns its length, allocating nothing;
`from_der(der)` reads strict DER back into the curve's `ecdsa_signature`;
`normalize_low_s(signature)` returns the low-S form, and `invalid_signature`
for an r or an s that is 0 or not below n; `is_low_s(signature)` is true
exactly when r is between 1 and n - 1 and s between 1 and (n - 1) / 2, and
does not verify.

secp256k1 keeps a third argument where it has a reason of its own: hedged
ECDSA, `sign(crypto::ecdsa_sha256, input, extra_entropy)`, mixes 32 fresh bytes
of the caller's into the nonce, and `sign(crypto::schnorr, message,
auxiliary_random_bytes)` takes BIP 340's auxiliary randomness from the caller,
for test vectors; `sign(crypto::schnorr, message)` draws it from
`random_device`, and returns `entropy_unavailable` when it cannot. `recover` takes the scheme and
the inputs of signing, `recover(crypto::ecdsa_sha256_recoverable, signature,
input)` or `recover(crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable, signature, input)`
(<<crypto-secp256k1-recovery>>).

Every `crypto::verify` takes its backend as its first template parameter,
`crypto::verify<Backend>(scheme, signature, input, public_key)`, so that the
call reads the same on every curve: P-256 deduces it from the key, Ed25519
takes its family's default, and secp256k1 libsecp256k1, its only backend.
`recover` and `async_verify` take it first too. Ed25519's verification policy
is a trailing argument, `curves::ed25519::strict` by default
(<<crypto-ed25519>>).

In an event loop, `async_sign(executor, key_pair, scheme, input, token)` and
`async_verify(executor, scheme, signature, input, public_key, token)`, and the
members `key_pair.async_sign` and `public_key.async_verify`, take the same
schemes and inputs on the curves WebCrypto has, Ed25519 and P-256, and check
them as `crypto::can_sign` and `crypto::can_verify` do
(<<crypto-curves-four-forms>>, <<crypto-async>>).

[#crypto-curves-keypair]
==== The key pair contract

`<webcpp/crypto/keypair.hpp>` declares three concepts, by what a key pair
does, each curve's header checking its own row of them with a `static_assert`
each. The concept `crypto::keypair`, which every curve's key pair meets:

* `from_secret(secret_bytes)` makes it from its secret, and `generate()` draws one
from `random_device`, each giving `result<K>`;
* `public_key()` gives `result<K::public_key_type>`, the curve's typed public
key, whose value goes straight into `crypto::verify`: `curves::ed25519::public_key`,
`curves::p256::public_key` or `curves::secp256k1::public_key`;
* `secret()` gives `result<secret<N>>`, the bytes `from_secret` takes, in a
`secret<N>` that wipes itself: Ed25519's seed, P-256's scalar, secp256k1's secret key. It is the
export a PKCS#8 file or a backup takes.

`crypto::signing_keypair` adds `sign(scheme, input)`, with every scheme its
curve has, at least one, each giving the scheme's signature type: Ed25519's,
P-256's and secp256k1's key pairs. `crypto::agreement_keypair` adds
`agree(peer)`, the peer its own typed public key, giving a `secret<N>`:
X25519's, P-256's and secp256k1's. `crypto::sign` and `async_sign` take a
signing key pair, `crypto::agree` and `async_agree` an agreement key pair, and
another stops the build saying what it does: an X25519 key pair agrees and
does not sign, an Ed25519 key pair signs and does not agree.

Each public key type is made from bytes by its own `from_bytes`, and gives
its byte forms as members named after the encoding: Ed25519's `bytes()` (32
bytes), P-256's `uncompressed()` (65), and secp256k1's `compressed()` (33),
`uncompressed()` (65) and `xonly()` (32). P-256's and secp256k1's
`from_bytes` check the point, as P-256's `from_coordinates(x, y)` does;
Ed25519's checks the size alone, whether the point is canonical and not of
small order being its verification policy's judgment.

ECDH is `agree(peer)` on every curve that has it, the peer a typed public key,
and gives a `secret<32>`: X25519's gives RFC 7748's shared secret
(<<crypto-x25519>>), and the others the shared point's raw x coordinate, as
WebCrypto's `deriveBits` does: P-256's (<<crypto-p256-ecdh>>) and secp256k1's,
whose two other forms keep names of their own, `agree_hashed` and
`agree_point` (<<crypto-secp256k1-ecdh>>). `async_agree` gives X25519's and
P-256's through a completion. Ed25519's `to_x25519()` is a conversion, not an
agreement (<<crypto-x25519>>).

[#crypto-curves-four-forms]
==== Four forms of every operation

An operation of a key pair, a public key and an AEAD has up to four forms and
one implementation: the member and its free form everywhere, and their
asynchronous forms where WebCrypto has the algorithm. The member does the work; its free form, in the namespace
`webcpp::crypto`, calls the member in one line (`crypto::verify` calls the
work the public key's `verify` calls); the asynchronous member and
its free form share one initiation, which runs the synchronous member when the
operation is initiated and completes through the event loop
(<<crypto-async>>). The forms take the same arguments and give the same bytes,
the same errors and the same refusals, one set of concepts gating them all, so a program picks the form
that reads best where it calls: the member beside the object, the free form in
generic code, an asynchronous form in an event loop.

.The four forms of each operation
[cols="1,4,4",options="header"]
|===
| | Synchronous | Asynchronous

| Member
| `K::from_secret(secret_bytes)`, `T::from_bytes(bytes)`, `C::from_key(key)`,
`K::generate()`, `key_pair.sign(scheme, input)`,
`key_pair.agree(peer)`, `public_key.verify(scheme, signature, input)`,
`aead.seal(nonce, associated_data, data)`,
`aead.open(nonce, associated_data, data, tag)`
| `K::async_from_secret(executor, secret_bytes, token)`, `C::async_from_key(executor, key,
token)`, `K::async_generate(executor, token)`,
`key_pair.async_sign(executor, scheme, input, token)`,
`key_pair.async_agree(executor, peer, token)`,
`public_key.async_verify(executor, scheme, signature, input, token)`,
`aead.async_seal(executor, nonce, associated_data, data, token)`,
`aead.async_open(executor, nonce, associated_data, data, tag, token)`

| Free, in `webcpp::crypto`
| `crypto::from_secret<K>(secret_bytes)`, `crypto::from_bytes<T>(bytes)`,
`crypto::from_key<C>(key)`, `crypto::generate<K>()`,
`crypto::sign(key_pair, scheme, input)`, `crypto::agree(key_pair, peer)`,
`crypto::verify(scheme, signature, input, public_key)`,
`crypto::seal(aead, nonce, associated_data, data)`,
`crypto::open(aead, nonce, associated_data, data, tag)`
| `crypto::async_from_secret<K>(executor, secret_bytes, token)`,
`crypto::async_from_key<C>(executor, key, token)`,
`crypto::async_generate<K>(executor, token)`,
`crypto::async_sign(executor, key_pair, scheme, input, token)`,
`crypto::async_agree(executor, key_pair, peer, token)`,
`crypto::async_verify(executor, scheme, signature, input, public_key, token)`,
`crypto::async_seal(executor, aead, nonce, associated_data, data, token)`,
`crypto::async_open(executor, aead, nonce, associated_data, data, tag, token)`
|===

The synchronous forms exist wherever the member does: on Ed25519's, P-256's
and secp256k1's key pairs and public keys (`agree` on P-256 and secp256k1),
and on AES-GCM and every AEAD of `ciphers::aead`, the ChaCha20-Poly1305 forms
and the secret boxes. The asynchronous forms exist for what WebCrypto has:
Ed25519's and P-256's key pairs and public keys (`async_agree` on P-256), and
AES-GCM. A public key has no `async_from_bytes`: the asynchronous family makes
key pairs (`async_from_secret`) and AES-GCM objects (`async_from_key`) only.
Ed25519's verification policy is an optional argument of all four, before the
token in the asynchronous ones; secp256k1's 32 bytes of extra entropy or
auxiliary randomness follow the input of `sign` and `crypto::sign`.

Each free synchronous form is declared in the header of what it gates:
`crypto::sign` and `crypto::agree` in `<webcpp/crypto/keypair.hpp>`, beside
the concepts that constrain them; the catch-all of `crypto::verify` in
`<webcpp/crypto/signing.hpp>`, whose overloads each curve's header declares;
`crypto::from_secret`,
`crypto::from_bytes`, `crypto::from_key` and `crypto::generate` in
`<webcpp/crypto/factories.hpp>`, which `keypair.hpp` and the
AEAD headers include; `crypto::seal` and `crypto::open` in
`<webcpp/crypto/ciphers/aead.hpp>`, which `gcm.hpp` includes. Every free form
holds its own timing guard before it calls the member, which holds one too,
except `crypto::from_bytes`: a public value's `from_bytes` holds a guard only
where it calls a backend (P-256's and secp256k1's check the point), and
Ed25519's `public_key::from_bytes`, which copies 32 bytes, holds none, as no
function without a backend call does, so the free form adds none either: a
guard there would only set DIT around a copy (<<crypto-backends-dit>>), at the
guard's own cost (<<crypto-performance-timing>>).

An unqualified call finds the free forms by argument-dependent lookup, a scheme
tag being of the namespace `webcpp::crypto`: `verify(crypto::eddsa, signature,
message, public_key)` is `crypto::verify`. The catch-alls that stop the build
with a message, those of `crypto::sign`, `crypto::verify` and
`crypto::async_verify`, take only a scheme tag in the place of the scheme, and
the one of `crypto::agree` only a key pair of the contract, so that a function
`sign` or `verify` of the program, of the same arity, called unqualified on the
program's own types, is never captured by one of them.

The asynchronous members are declared in their classes and defined in
`<webcpp/crypto/async.hpp>`, so that a curve's or a cipher's header never
includes Asio and a synchronous program pays nothing for them. A program that
calls one includes `async.hpp` and adds `<library>/webcpp/asio//asio` to its
Jamfile, as for the free asynchronous forms. Without the header the call does
not compile, and the compiler says that the member's deduced return type is
used before it is defined: clang's "function ... with deduced return type
cannot be used before it is defined", gcc's "use of ... before deduction of
'auto'", MSVC's C3779; the message and its fix are in
<<crypto-jamfile-messages>>. On a backend that completes by itself, as the
browser's will be (<<crypto-async-webcrypto>>), every synchronous form refuses
at compile time with a message that names its `async_` form: the refusal is in
place already, tested with a stand-in backend, since no such backend exists
yet.

[#crypto-curves-signatures-bitcoin]
==== Bitcoin's sighash

A Bitcoin legacy or segwit v0 input signs its sighash, the SHA-256 of the
SHA-256 of the transaction's serialization
(link:https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0143.mediawiki[BIP
143]): a protocol's digest. crypto holds primitives only, so the program, or a
domain library, computes the sighash with webcpp::hash and signs
`+crypto::prehashed{sighash}+` under `crypto::ecdsa_sha256`. That is honest: the
sighash is the SHA-256 of a SHA-256, the second hash being the scheme's own,
and no scheme of its own is needed:

[source,cpp]
----
include::{examples}/bitcoin_sighash.cpp[tag=sign,indent=0]
----

The sighash's computation, and the signature written as DER, are in
<<crypto-wallets-der>>. A taproot sighash, BIP 341's tagged hash, is the
message of `crypto::schnorr` instead, signed as given
(<<crypto-wallets-taproot>>, <<crypto-secp256k1-musig>>).

[#crypto-curves-signatures-ethereum]
==== Ethereum's digest

Ethereum signs Keccak-256 digests with recoverable ECDSA
(link:https://ethereum.github.io/yellowpaper/paper.pdf[Ethereum's yellow
paper, appendix F]), and its scheme says so: `crypto::ecdsa_keccak256_recoverable`
hashes a message with Keccak-256, the original padding Ethereum uses and never
SHA3-256, and signs a digest the program builds, EIP-191's, EIP-712's or a
transaction's, given as `+crypto::prehashed{digest}+`. Its signature is the
`curves::secp256k1::recoverable_signature` of `crypto::ecdsa_sha256_recoverable`,
and a message recovered under the other scheme, hashed with the other hash,
gives another key, which the program's comparison refuses. <<crypto-secp256k1-ethereum>> shows the digest, `v` and the
address, which the program computes.
